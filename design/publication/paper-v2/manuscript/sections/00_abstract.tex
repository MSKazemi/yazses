\begin{abstract}
Offline speech recognition has improved to the point that practical voice input can run entirely
on commodity CPUs, avoiding the privacy and connectivity costs of cloud transcription. However,
the usefulness of an interactive dictation system depends on more than a single word-error-rate
measurement on clean speech. Model-specific tail failures, decoder configuration, hardware
variation, audio difficulty, and task-specific post-processing can materially affect user-visible
behavior while remaining hidden by corpus-average accuracy.

We present an expanded empirical evaluation of \YazSes, a privacy-first offline voice-input
system. Building on an earlier evaluation limited primarily to three Whisper checkpoints on clean
LibriSpeech speech and one Linux laptop, we evaluate eight local engine/checkpoint configurations,
clean and harder speech conditions, repeated decoding, four operating-system/ISA environments,
interaction-specific latency mechanisms, and real annotated meetings. On a controlled Azure CPU
host, Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 produced the lowest \wer{} point estimate on the measured
200-utterance clean subset at 2.06\%, compared with 2.66\% for Whisper \texttt{small.en},
while Moonshine/tiny produced the lowest measured \rtf{} at 0.016. On the corresponding harder
split, Parakeet increased to 2.88\%, while several other configurations approximately doubled
their error rate.

Repeated decoding exposed a distinct failure mode in Whisper \texttt{large-v3}. Across repeated
runs of the same hard-speech subset, substitutions remained fixed at 87, deletions at 15, and
correct-word hits at 3,619, while insertions varied from 101 to 184. Thus the observed run-to-run
\wer{} variation was driven by extra emitted text rather than changes in recognition
substitutions. Follow-up experiments further show that previous-text conditioning has
checkpoint-dependent effects: it benefits smaller checkpoints in the measured conditions, is
byte-identical at \texttt{medium.en}, and is associated with rare runaway continuation in
\texttt{large-v3}.

The broader evaluation also changes several system-level conclusions. Synthetic leading silence
does not recover speech that was never captured; CPU streaming is not a general latency
improvement; cross-platform numerical variation is checkpoint-dependent; and a diarization
clustering threshold that appeared reasonable on short or synthetic audio produced 75.21\% mean
diarization error rate on the 16-recording AMI test split. Recalibration reduced mean \der{} to
26.71\%, while experiments with known speaker count showed that obtaining the correct number of
speakers and reducing \der{} are distinct objectives.

These results suggest that offline interactive speech systems should report reproducibility,
tail failures, hardware dependence, and task-specific metrics alongside conventional aggregate
\wer{}.
\end{abstract}
